              Looping basics

              Beats, bars and loops

              A beat is one tap of your foot. A bar is a group of beats, usually four: 1-2-3-4. A loop is a few bars that repeat forever. Here every loop is 1, 2, 4 or 8 bars long, so they always line up with each other.

              How long should a loop be?

              • 1 bar is a groove: a beatbox pattern, or a "dum-dum" bass you repeat.
              • 2 bars is a riff: a bassline or rhythm with a little call and answer.
              • 4 bars is the sweet spot: a chord pattern or a short tune. Most songs think in fours.
              • 8 bars is a whole phrase, like a verse melody. Lovely, but harder to nail in one go, so try 4 first.

              A good mix: one longer loop as the backbone, shorter loops for rhythm on top.

              Build it in layers

              1. Bass: a low hum or "bom… bom".
              2. Rhythm: mouth percussion, clicks, "ts" and "k" sounds.
              3. Harmony: long "oohs" and "aahs" that sit underneath.
              4. A hook: a short tune people would hum back.
              5. Sing on top: words, riffs, anything. This part doesn't loop, so it can wander.

              Leave space

              Yes, space is your friend. Every layer adds up, so keep each one simple and leave gaps; a rest in one loop is room for another. Use different heights, low, middle and high: the pitch strip on the right of each pad shows where it sits, so spread them out. If it sounds muddy, take a loop out rather than adding another.

              Timing

              Before recording, a hand moves slowly round the pad at the same pace as your loops, through the last bar before the top. Come in right as it reaches the red mark at the top, when the ring turns red. The ring turns white for the last bar: that's your cue to finish your phrase and take a breath. Slightly early or late? Tap the pad's ⚙ → Trim and slide it into place while it plays with the others. Headphones stop the loops leaking into the mic.

              Make it a song

              Songs come from loops coming in and dropping out. Try: one loop → build up → everything → drop to one or two → all back in → end on one. Tap pads to bring them in and out while ● Rec or Sing on top records it.

              What's "eight-track"?

              Before computers, studios recorded onto tape machines with separate tracks. An 8-track could record eight parts separately, then mix them together. Your eight pads work the same way: eight separate parts you can bring in and out. (There was also the 1970s 8-track cartridge, a tape for car stereos.)

                Getting started

                1. Headphones help, so the loops don't leak into your mic (wired ones are best: Bluetooth adds a little delay). No headphones? Switch the other loops off while you record, then line the new one up afterwards (step 4).
                2. Tap a pad. It starts together with any loops playing: a hand moves round at the same pace as your loops; start humming as it reaches the red mark at the top. Try a low “bom… bom” first.
                3. Line up with a loop: watch the pad you want to match. Its playhead comes round to the top just as yours starts, so you can come in close.
                4. A little off? Tap the pad's ⚙ → Trim & silence. Pick another pad under Compare with to see the shapes of both sounds, then slide the whole loop into sync, or use Move a part to shift just one note. It plays with your other loops while you do it, and you can silence a breath there too.
                5. Add layers on other pads: a beat, then a tune. Tap pads to bring them in and out.
                6. Tap Sing on top or ● Rec to record a take, then ♫ to save it as an MP3.
